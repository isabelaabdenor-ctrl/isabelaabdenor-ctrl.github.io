An app for agencies and clients to approve creative work

In Brazil, ads and social posts are approved over WhatsApp and email, and nobody is sure which version counts. I designed an app to fix that, tested it with 5 people and found that telling people the piece changed isn't enough: you have to tell them what happens to their approval.

30-second summary

The problem
Creative work is approved over WhatsApp and email, and nobody is sure which version counts or who approved it.
What I found
Saying the piece changed isn't enough. Even with the notice on screen, 4 of 5 people didn't know whether their old approval still counted.
What I proposed
Review by link with no account needed, comments pinned to the exact spot on the piece, an approval receipt and a clear rule: an approval only applies to the version approved.

Context

Why this problem

I know this problem first-hand from art direction: the piece goes out on WhatsApp, comes back with "change that color" and turns into "final_v3_FORREAL.png".

And it isn't just my impression. In a study of 1,034 marketing and agency professionals from several countries:

  • 5 roundson average before an idea is approved, up from 3 in 2007
  • 43%of agencies say the right people are the ones approving
  • 30%of agencies get clear feedback from the client
The limit of these numbersThe study measures the approval of campaign ideas, not of each piece, and it isn't Brazil-only. I use the numbers to show the size of the problem, not as a target for the product.

In Brazil, this conversation happens on WhatsApp: 97% of users open the app every day, and 82% have already talked to businesses through it.

The research path, in 4 steps

  1. What I assumed"Brazilian clients only approve over WhatsApp."
  2. InterviewsThe channel changes from client to client. I was only half right.
  3. Prototype testThe change notice was on screen, and still 4 of 5 were unsure.
  4. The findingIt's not enough to say the piece changed. You have to say what happens to the approval.

Step 1

I talked to people who send and people who approve

I interviewed 5 people from both sides: an agency designer, a freelance designer, an account manager, a marketing manager and a communications coordinator. I asked each of them to walk me through, start to finish, the last piece they approved or sent for approval.

All 5 conversations showed the same four problems:

  1. Feedback arrives scatteredWhatsApp, email, voice notes, calls, meetings. At the agency, someone becomes a translator: "My job is to gather everything and turn it into a request the creative team can understand."
  2. Nobody knows which version countsThe "final" one is guessed from the file name or the last message. It has already been costly: a piece went out with the old price.
  3. It's unclear who has the final saySomeone else changes what had already been approved: "We made two versions without knowing which direction to follow."
  4. Tools get abandonedWhen the client has to create an account to approve, they go back to messaging.
I was only half rightI thought the problem was Brazilian clients only approving over WhatsApp. But clients at large companies approved over email and Teams. The channel changes; what repeats is the lack of a reliable record of which version was approved, and by whom.

Step 2

I looked at what already exists

Before designing, I checked how two well-known approval tools, Filestage and Frame.io, handle this. I read the public help documentation of both.

Explains what happens to an approval when a new version is uploadedNot found in the docsNot found in the docsYes, it's the focus

In other words: approving without an account wouldn't be a differentiator, since both already do it. The open space was making clear what counts when the piece changes.

Even the biggest stumble on the linkOn Frame.io's official forum there are 2025 reports of clients who couldn't comment through the review link. The moment the client opens the link is the most fragile point of the whole process.

The challenge

How might we know which version was approved, and by whom?

And in a way that lets the client approve right on the piece, through a link, without creating an account. For the first version of the app, I picked what solved the four problems with the least effort for the client:

In

  • A review link with no login, to send on WhatsApp and open on a phone.
  • Comments on the exact spot of the piece, saved on the version where they were made.
  • One designated approver: others comment, only one approves.
  • An approval badge showing who approved and when.
  • A notice when the piece changes after being approved.

Left for later

  • Review by department, like legal and sales.
  • Comparing two versions side by side.
  • Importing comments from WhatsApp.

They matter, but they're expensive. First, the app needed to prove that clients actually use the link.

Step 3

Prototype and test with 5 people

I built a clickable prototype in Figma with 22 screens, still in grayscale. That was on purpose: I wanted to test the flow before working on the visuals. The screens are in Portuguese.

  • ReviewThe client opens the link on a phone.

  • CommentThe request is pinned to a spot on the piece.

  • SentThe new version arrives on the same link.

  • ChangedThe first change notice.

I tested with 5 people who hadn't taken part in the interviews: 3 from the agency side and 2 from the client side. After each task, the person rated how easy it was from 1 to 7. The goal was an average of 5 or more on every task.

The main flow worked. The two scores below the goal happened at the same moment: when the piece changes after being approved. The issues that came up:

  • 4 of 5didn't know whether the old approval still counted after the change
  • 4 of 5struggled to tell the approved version from the newest one
  • 2 of 5missed a clear confirmation that the piece was approved
"The most confusing part was understanding whether the previous approval was still valid after a change."Test participant, client side (translated from Portuguese)

The finding

The change notice was already on screen. What was missing was saying what it meant for the approval.

Limits of this testThe agency tasks were adapted from the original script, and task time wasn't measured. With 5 people, I write "4 of 5", not percentages.

Solution

The redesign

The problem didn't call for a new feature. It called for the screen to state the consequence, not just the fact. Drag the slider to compare the notice before and after the test.

Before After
  • BeforeA dashed strip, with the same weight as the rest of the screen, said the piece had changed. It didn't say what to do.
  • AfterThe notice becomes the screen's heading and states the consequence: "Your approval of v3 does not apply to v4. Review and approve again."

What changed

  1. The screen says which version you're viewing"You're viewing: v4 · not approved" (v4 is the piece's 4th version), with tabs to open each version.
  2. Both versions appear togetherv4 awaiting approval and v3 approved, with a note that it "does not apply to v4".
  3. The history became a timelineWho did what, when, and the state of each version: current, approved or replaced.
  4. The approval became a receiptPiece, version, who approved, when and a code. The agency gets notified and can download the approved version.

Step 4

How I validated without new people

I couldn't recruit new people to test again. Instead of skipping this step, I switched methods:

  • Heuristic evaluation: I reviewed the screens against Nielsen's heuristics, a classic list of usability best practices.
  • Step-by-step walkthrough (the technical name is cognitive walkthrough): I redid the two failed tasks as if I were the person using it, in the old version and in the new one.

The evaluation found something the test didn't: in the 1st version, when a new version was uploaded, the dashboard still said "Approved". The agency could publish a file nobody had approved. It was a state I simply hadn't designed.

It also found issues in the redesign itself. The main one: with a new version pending, the agency couldn't download the approved version. In the final version I fixed that, gave each status color a single meaning and adjusted text that had too little contrast to read comfortably.

The limit of this methodThe recommendation is 3 to 5 evaluators. Here it was just me, and I also designed the screens. The evaluation doesn't replace a new test with people, which is still the next step.

Interface

The final UI

With the flow validated, I designed the final version starting from one question: what does color need to say?

  • ReviewThe piece comes first.

  • Comment on a spotOne main action: send.

  • Changed after approvalThe only strong yellow in the app.

  • ReceiptThe approval is on record.

  • The piece is the star. Whoever approves is there to look at the piece, which is the most colorful element on screen. Everything else stays neutral and only uses color when color means something.
  • The 60-30-10 rule. About 60% of the screen is a neutral background, 30% is moss green (text and main button) and 10% is accents. Because accents are rare, they stand out.
  • Each color has one meaning, and never appears alone. Green with ✓ means approved; yellow, someone needs to act; dashed, it's the other side's turn; gray, it's now history.
The yellow mistakeIn the first draft, the "v4 awaiting" block used the same yellow as the price tag on the piece, and the two competed for attention. I kept the strong yellow only for the notice to the client.

Takeaways

What I learned

Information on the screen is not the same as information understood. The notice was there. It lacked emphasis, and it didn't say what it meant.

The states nobody designs are where products break. The dashboard saying "Approved" for a version nobody approved came from a screen I hadn't anticipated.

When you can't use the ideal method, switch methods and say so. It's better than skipping validation or pretending it happened.

Next steps

  • Test again with 2 or 3 new people, only on the two tasks below the goal. Target: a score of 5 or more.
  • Several approvals at once. Today the dashboard notice shows a single piece. The next version would group them: "3 pieces approved today".
  • What I'd measure after launch: how many revision rounds each piece takes (the market average is 5), how many approvals happen through the link and how many days pass between sending and approval.
